Embracing JOMO (Joy of Missing Out)

Published by Aura Journal • August 2026

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from trying to keep up with everything. Every event, every conversation, every new trend, and every update seems to demand a little piece of our attention. Even during moments meant for rest, the possibility that something interesting is happening elsewhere can make it difficult to feel fully present.

This is where the joy of missing out, often called JOMO, offers a different perspective. Rather than treating every missed invitation or unread update as a loss, JOMO encourages us to appreciate the experiences we choose instead. It is the quiet satisfaction of being comfortable with our own decisions, even when other possibilities remain available.

Understanding the Pressure to Keep Up

Modern technology makes it easy to see what other people are doing at almost any moment. Photographs of celebrations, travel experiences, career milestones, and everyday achievements can create the impression that everyone else is constantly experiencing something remarkable.

When we repeatedly compare our ordinary moments with carefully selected glimpses of other people's lives, our own experiences can begin to feel insufficient. A peaceful evening at home may seem unremarkable beside a friend's weekend away, even when that quiet evening is exactly what we need.

The problem is not necessarily curiosity or enjoying other people's updates. It begins when the fear of missing something starts influencing our choices more than our actual interests, priorities, or energy levels.

"A fuller life does not require attending every moment happening around you. Sometimes, it begins with appreciating the moment already in your hands."

From Fear of Missing Out to Freedom of Choice

The shift toward JOMO begins with recognising that every choice naturally excludes other possibilities. Spending an evening reading means not attending an event. Taking a weekend to rest means declining some invitations. Concentrating on a personal project means allowing other distractions to wait.

These trade-offs are not automatically mistakes. They are part of directing limited time and attention toward the things that matter most. Once we accept that no one can experience everything, it becomes easier to make choices without constantly wondering whether another option would have been better.

JOMO is not about avoiding people or rejecting new experiences. It is about choosing participation deliberately instead of responding to every invitation, notification, or expectation out of anxiety.

Creating More Space Away from Screens

Digital habits can make the fear of missing out feel especially persistent. A quick check of a social feed can turn into an extended period of scrolling, with one update leading to another. Even when nothing urgent needs our attention, the habit of checking can become difficult to interrupt.

Creating a little distance from these patterns does not require abandoning technology. Small boundaries can help restore a healthier relationship with it.

  • Choose specific checking times: Instead of opening social apps automatically throughout the day, set a few intentional moments to catch up.
  • Protect device-free activities: Give meals, walks, conversations, and bedtime some space without constant notifications.
  • Review unnecessary alerts: Turn off notifications that repeatedly interrupt you without providing meaningful value.
  • Notice your reasons: Before opening an app, pause briefly and ask whether you have a purpose or are simply responding to habit.

These small adjustments create opportunities to experience the present without repeatedly comparing it with what might be happening elsewhere.

Learning to Enjoy an Unscheduled Evening

An open evening can initially feel uncomfortable if we are used to filling every available hour. Without a plan, we may reach for our phones or search for something productive to do, as though unstructured time needs to be justified.

Yet an unscheduled evening can become an opportunity to rediscover simple pleasures. You might prepare a meal slowly, listen to music, organise a favourite corner of your home, read a few pages, or enjoy a conversation without watching the clock.

The activity itself does not need to be impressive. What matters is that you are choosing it because it feels worthwhile to you, rather than because it creates something to display or report to others.

Making Peace with Other People's Choices

Embracing JOMO also means allowing other people to enjoy experiences that are different from our own. Friends may travel more often, attend more gatherings, pursue different ambitions, or spend their free time in ways we would not choose.

Their decisions do not need to become a measure of our own lives. Someone else's enjoyable weekend does not make your restful one less meaningful. Another person's achievement does not invalidate the progress you are making at your own pace.

When comparison becomes less central, it becomes easier to celebrate other people without feeling that their experiences create an obligation for us to do more.

Building a Life Around What Matters to You

It is easier to decline distractions when you have a clearer sense of what deserves your attention. This does not require a perfect life plan or a fixed set of long-term goals. Even a few simple priorities can help you recognise which invitations and activities genuinely fit your current needs.

You might value time with family, creative work, physical activity, learning, or simply having enough space to recover after a demanding week. These priorities can change over time, and that is perfectly natural. The purpose is not to create another rigid system to follow, but to make everyday decisions feel more intentional.

Occasionally ask yourself what you would like more room for in your life. Then consider whether your habits reflect that answer. The difference between a crowded schedule and a meaningful one often lies in how deliberately that space is used.

Finding Contentment in What You Choose

JOMO develops through ordinary decisions rather than dramatic lifestyle changes. It may mean leaving your phone in another room, declining an invitation without overexplaining, or allowing a quiet afternoon to remain quiet.

There will still be moments of curiosity, comparison, and uncertainty. The aim is not to eliminate these feelings entirely. It is to notice them without automatically allowing them to dictate your next action.

Over time, choosing where to place your attention can become less about keeping up with everyone else and more about being present in the life you are creating.

"You do not have to be everywhere to live meaningfully. You only have to be present where you choose to be."

In a culture that often celebrates constant participation, there is something quietly powerful about knowing when enough is enough. Missing an experience does not always mean losing something valuable. Sometimes, it means making room for something you would otherwise overlook.

The joy of missing out begins when you stop asking whether you are keeping up with everything and start appreciating the things you have chosen to keep close.
